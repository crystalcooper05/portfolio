ME 315 Machine Design | Spring 2026

Two-Stage Gearbox

CAD model of the two-stage gearbox

Gearbox diagram

Shaft B assembly

My team designed and analyzed a two-stage gear reduction system for an industrial production line, transmitting 70 kW from motor to output while keeping the input and output shafts close together, ensuring a 5+ year service life, and optimizing for cost and manufacturability.

I led the design of Shaft B, the most complex component because it carries two gears at once, creating dual load paths and requiring careful coordination of alignment and stress. I chose spur gears over helical alternatives, accepting slightly higher noise in exchange for lower cost and simpler manufacturing.

For bearings, our initial selection could not meet the 5+ year service life, so I switched Shaft B to SKF 6313 deep-groove bearings and planned one replacement during the product's life.

Through stress analysis and fatigue calculations, the final design achieves infinite fatigue life across all shafts and gears, with a minimum gear bending safety factor of 1.36 and a Shaft B Goodman safety factor of 2.68.

ME 240 Mechanical Design | Spring 2025

3D-Printed Brake Calipers

Final prototype on a bicycle

Topology optimization results

FEA stress analysis

First prototype

My group designed 3D-printed bicycle brake calipers that meet ISO 4210, EN 14781, and CPSC requirements while optimizing for manufacturability and performance. The design had to balance braking distance, deflection, stress, mass, and fit within the space around the frame, all in Nylon-12, a relatively brittle material.

I translated the safety standards into design parameters, then ran two rounds of topology optimization and iterative FEA to minimize mass under a 2.75 mm deflection constraint. The optimization pushed material toward the vertical load paths and rounded transitions.

Peak stress dropped from 150 MPa to 21 MPa (an 86% reduction), and deflection dropped 7.5% in the redesign. The final calipers reached a 12.2 m stopping distance, down from 15.0 m, and met all marginal performance targets, though not the 10 m ideal.

DTC 2 Design Thinking | Spring 2024

SmartReach

Final prototype

In use

Mockup testing with user

User observation session

My team designed an extendable pointer that helps wheelchair users with limited upper-body mobility press ADA push-to-open buttons at public establishments. We worked directly with Sophia, a 20-year-old with cerebral palsy, to address a core barrier: ADA buttons on her right side force wheelchair repositioning, and doors often close before she can pass through. Existing telescopic pointers failed because they demanded precision and grip strength she lacked.

I led user research and iterative design over a 9-week testing cycle, conducting on-site observations at dining establishments and testing six mockups directly with Sophia. Early iterations explored wrist attachments and bracelet designs, but testing showed they caused discomfort and restricted her natural reach. We pivoted to an accordion-fold mechanism deployed by a trigger, which she found easier than pressing a button, and kept the accordion-fold with magnetic attachment after comparing it against expandable tips and lap-mounted designs.

Testing revealed a counter-intuitive insight that reshaped the design: a wide, flat pointer tip, though less elegant, was far more effective than a precision-pointed tip because it reduced the cognitive and motor precision required. This taught me that accessibility design means setting aside assumptions about "optimal" solutions in favor of what users actually need.

The final design lets Sophia press buttons on her own, without repositioning her wheelchair or relying on a caregiver. Elastic armrest sleeves and a bungee cord support independent use and protect the device from drops.

DSGN 380-1 Industrial Design | Spring 2026

Flat-Pack Vertical Utensil Organizer

Final prototype

CAD model

Cardboard mockup

Laser-cut plywood parts

I designed a flat-pack vertical utensil organizer to solve a problem in my own kitchen: limited drawer space and plenty of counter space. Inspired by vertical spoon stands, I wanted a modular storage system that frees up drawer space for food while keeping everyday utensils within reach.

I explored several sketch concepts before settling on a tiered organizer with a central vertical spine, side compartments for larger utensils and spatulas, and a top shelf. I prototyped in stages: first a cardboard and wood hybrid to test the basic form, then a paper-based composite from the shop. That material was too flimsy, so I moved to plywood for precision and durability while keeping the profile narrow.

Laser cutting brought tolerance problems. My first prototype cut too tight and the pieces would not fit together, and the second was too loose. Instead of re-sketching, I built tolerance parameters into my Onshape CAD model and adjusted them systematically across three laser-cutter trials. Embedding tolerance in the model meant each refinement took minutes in CAD instead of hours of rework.

I chose 1/8-inch plywood because the laser cutter is more precise with thinner material, waste is lower, and the finished piece stays compact, which fits the goal of saving counter space. The final prototype holds utensils securely while keeping the vertical storage that inspired the project. The main lesson was that constraints drive better design: limited drawer space was the insight behind the solution.

Design, Thinking, and Communication | Winter 2024

The Printato: Hand Printing Press

Team poster

Final prototype

In use

Interchangeable attachments

Lever dimensions

The Printato is a hand printing press built from household objects, made for prints where variation becomes part of the art. Our client, Dr. Alice Boone, asked for a press that could be made at home and still leave room for error and creativity, instead of producing identical prints.

User observations showed that pressing by hand took a lot of force and produced uneven pressure and inconsistent placement. We used a potato masher as the stamp, since its head spreads force evenly. A hinged lever with an extendable rail controls where the print lands, and a lazy susan base lets the user rotate the lever 360 degrees to print anywhere on the paper.

Velcro on the stamp lets users swap attachments, from bubble wrap to cardboard to any texture they stick on, and the velcro pieces come off for cleaning. We built the base board with a square grid on its wipe-down cover so users can line up paper with the press on purpose, and the cover catches paint so mess stays easy to clean after user observations showed it was a concern. Mockup testing showed that the rotating base made the press more versatile and easier to use. I led the design process, from ideation and user observations through mockup testing and the final prototype.
